# Research log — Cyrus Cylinder first pass (2026-10-28)

Verified against live pages on 2026-10-28. Each source is recorded with **the specific claim it supports**. Items that could not be opened as full texts are listed as inaccessible, not paraphrased from memory.

Related files: [FOUNDATIONAL_PHILOSOPHY.md](./FOUNDATIONAL_PHILOSOPHY.md), `src/data/research/`.

## Method

- Prefer museum catalogs, encyclopaedias, and named editions over viral posts.
- Quote or reproduce a translation only when the license is verified.
- Keep three layers separate: historical evidence, philosophical interpretation, modern cultural meaning.
- If a source disputes a popular claim, record the dispute rather than picking a side.

## 1. British Museum collection — BM 90920

- **URL:** https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_1880-0617-1941
- **Accessed:** 2026-10-28 (live catalog)
- **License of the catalog text:** British Museum website content; not treated as freely reproducible inscription translation.
- **Image on the catalog page:** museum preview JPEG; no reusable license stated on the object page. **Not used.**

**Claims this source supports (historical evidence):**

- Object title “The Cyrus Cylinder”; museum number **90920**.
- Object type: fired-clay cylinder, wheel-made.
- Cultures/periods: Achaemenid Dynasty; production date **539 BC (after)**; made in Babylon (historic Iraq).
- Excavator: **Hormuzd Rassam**. Findspot: **Amran (Babylon)**; catalog points to Walker 1972a for findspot evidence.
- Dimensions: length **21.90–22.80 cm**; maximum diameter **10 cm**; end A diameter 7.80–8.20 cm; end B 7.90 cm.
- Inscription language listed as **Babylonian**; script **cuneiform**.
- Catalog description: a Babylonian account of Cyrus’s conquest of Babylon in 539 BC; restoration to temples of statues removed by Nabonidus; Cyrus’s own work at Babylon. Typical Late Babylonian royal / foundation cylinder, deposited in the city wall after the capture of Babylon.
- Incomplete: found in fragments; even after restoration the text is incomplete; unusually large grey stone inclusions in the core.
- The catalog publishes a **“New translation by Irving Finkel”**. That translation is a BM/Finkel text. **We do not reproduce it** (copyright). We link to the catalog.
- Bibliography on the object includes Rawlinson & Pinches 1884, Rassam 1897, Weissbach 1911, Nies & Keiser 1920 (Yale fragment), Pritchard/ANET, Walker 1972, Kuhrt 1983, **Finkel 2013a** (“definitive account of the text…”), Simpson 2013 (display and replica).
- Related BM object: clay tablet fragment that duplicates lines 1–2 and 42–45 of the Cylinder (catalog W_1881-0830-656), showing the text also existed in tablet copies.

**Does not support:** calling the object a modern human-rights charter; any measurement other than the ranges above; a downloadable official 3D scan (none is offered on this page).

## 2. Encyclopaedia Iranica — “Cyrus iv. The Cyrus cylinder”

- **URL:** https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/cyrus-iv/
- **Author:** Muhammad A. Dandamayev
- **Published:** 15 December 1993 (Vol. VI, fasc. 5, pp. 521–522); last updated 26 July 2013
- **Accessed:** 2026-10-28

**Claims this source supports:**

- BM no. 90920; fragmentary clay cylinder; **Akkadian** inscription.
- Discovered in a **foundation deposit** by A. H. Rassam at the site of the **Marduk temple in Babylon in 1879** (Walker citing Rassam’s letter of 20 November 1879).
- A second fragment, lines **36–45**, identified in the Yale Babylonian collection (Nies and Keiser no. 32) by **P.-R. Berger**. Total **45 lines**; first three almost entirely broken away; incomplete at the end.
- Contents outline: Marduk on Nabonidus’s crimes (4–8); Marduk chooses Cyrus and Babylon falls without a battle (9–19); Cyrus in the first person, titles and genealogy (20–22); peace of the country (22–26); blessing of Cyrus and Cambyses (26–30); restoration of neglected cult and permission for exiled peoples to return (30–36); restoration of Babylon’s defenses and an inscription of Ashurbanipal (36–45).
- Composition: “actually composed by priests of Marduk,” in an archaizing form inspired by Neo-Assyrian models, especially Assurbanipal inscriptions drafted in Babylon (Harmatta). Typical Mesopotamian **building inscription** placed as a foundation deposit (Walker, p. 159).
- Bibliography includes Berger 1975, Harmatta 1971/1974, Kuhrt 1983, Oppenheim in ANET, Rawlinson 1884, Walker 1972, Weissbach 1911.

**Does not support:** treating the Cylinder as Cyrus’s autograph legal code or as a complete text.

## 3. Irving Finkel’s edition (2013)

- **Citation verified from BM bibliography and publisher listing:** Irving L. Finkel (ed.), *The Cyrus Cylinder: The King of Persia’s Proclamation from Ancient Babylon*, I.B. Tauris, London, 2013. ISBN **978-1780760636**. 160 pages.
- **BM catalog** calls this the “definitive account of the text with discovery, display history and modern resonances” (Finkel 2013a).
- **Access this pass:** bibliographic facts and the existence of Finkel’s translation on the BM object page. **Full book not accessed** (paywalled / copyrighted). **Finkel’s running translation is not reproduced here.**
- **Claim it supports at catalog level only:** there is a current British Museum scholarly edition/translation of 2013 that should be the reference edition for line readings; popular or 1912 translations are not a substitute for it.

Related later news (not opened as a primary Finkel publication here): reports that Finkel identified tablet copies of the proclamation in the BM (2010) and discussed inscribed horse bones in Beijing. Those claims are **not** used as established fact in the first release without the 2013 book or a BM catalog note in hand.

## 4. CAIS page (one version among several)

- **URL:** https://www.cais-soas.com/CAIS/History/hakhamaneshian/Cyrus-the-great/cyrus_cylinder_complete.htm
- **Accessed:** 2026-10-28
- **What it is:** Circle of Ancient Iranian Studies HTML page titled “Cyrus the Great' Cylinder — TRANSLITERATION & TRANSLATION OF THE TEXT,” with the popular heading “The World's First Charter of the Human Rights.”
- **Stated textual source:** transcription/translation of lines 1–36 “follows Gösta Ahlström’s adaptation in *The History of Ancient Palestine* (Minneapolis, 1993), derived from Robert William Rogers’s *Cuneiform Parallels to the Old Testament* (Eugene, 1912), and reproduced by K. C. Hanson. The translation is credited to Rogers.”
- **Persian PDF:** link to a Persian version “translated by Dr Shahrokh Razmju” on CAIS. **Not reused** (CAIS copyright policy for the page file; Razmju translation license not independently verified).
- **Copyright notice on the page:** CAIS 1998, UK CDPA 1988 s.77; educational read-only; ownership remains with author/source. **We do not copy CAIS HTML.**
- **Catalog errors on the CAIS fact box (do not follow):** place of discovery listed as **Nineveh**; size “23 cm long, 11 cm wide”; date 538 BCE. These conflict with the British Museum catalog (Amran/Babylon; length 21.90–22.80 cm; max diameter 10 cm; after 539 BC). **BM + Iranica win.**

**Claims this source supports:** that a widely circulated English/Akkadian presentation exists online, derived from **Rogers 1912**, covering the main body (fragment A, lines 1–36), and that CAIS frames the object as a human-rights charter. The latter is **modern cultural meaning / popular reception**, not historical evidence.

## 5. Rogers 1912 (public-domain English of fragment A)

- Robert William Rogers, *Cuneiform Parallels to the Old Testament*, New York: Eaton & Mains / London: Oxford University Press, 1912. Cylinder text at **pp. 380–384** (as cited by CAIS/Hanson).
- **Copy verified:** Internet Archive `cuneiformparalle00rogerich` (publication date 1912; copyright notice 1912). US publication more than 95 years ago: **public domain in the United States**.
- **Limits:** fragment A only (Yale fragment B was identified later, Berger 1975). Line readings are those of 1912, not Finkel 2013 or Schaudig 2001.
- **Use in this project:** short, clearly labeled English excerpts of fragment A, plus a Persian rendering marked as *our rendering of Rogers 1912, not a museum translation*. Not called “the official British Museum translation.”

## 7. Persepolis administrative archives

### 7.1 Iranica hub — “Persepolis Administrative Archives”

- **URL:** https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/persepolis-admin-archive/
- **Published:** 9 June 2017 (hub page)
- **Accessed:** 2026-10-28
- **Supports:** two groups of clay tablets, fragments, and sealings from administrative agencies at Persepolis, named for findspots: Fortification Archive and Treasury Archive; other sealings in the fortification wall may belong to related documents.
- Hub page is an index; detailed labor/ration claims below come from the older Iranica article actually read in full.

### 7.2 Iranica — “Persepolis Elamite Tablets”

- **URL:** https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/persepolis-elamite-tablets/
- **Author:** Muhammad A. Dandamayev
- **Published:** 20 July 2002; last updated 8 November 2012
- **Accessed:** 2026-10-28

**Claims this source supports:**

- Two archives excavated by the Oriental Institute of the University of Chicago, 1933–34 and 1936–38, from the reigns of Darius I, Xerxes, and Artaxerxes I.
- **Fortification tablets:** found in the Fortification area; over 30,000 tablets/fragments; **2,120** edited by Richard T. Hallock (1969; 1978) at the time of the article, many still unpublished. Dates: Darius I years 13–28 = **509–494 BCE**. Elamite; 44 of Hallock’s texts with Aramaic glosses. Record distribution of foodstuffs, management of flocks, provisioning of workers (*kurtaš*) and travelers, transport of commodities, state reserves/seed, journals of barley, flour, dates, fruit, beer, etc., and receipt of livestock and grain as **royal taxes**.
- **Treasury tablets:** northeastern room of the Treasury of Xerxes; 753 tablets/fragments, **128** published (Cameron 1948, 1958, 1965). Dates: Darius I year 30 to Artaxerxes I year 7 = **492–458 BCE**. Record issue of **silver and foodstuffs** primarily to workmen of the royal economy at Persepolis and suburbs; named groups include Cappadocians, Lydians, Carians, Thracians, Ionians, Sogdians, Bactrians, Babylonians, Egyptians.
- Limits: large unpublished remainder; many Treasury fragments too worn for connected text; this is **royal-economy administration**, not a law code of private property. Estates of royal-family members appear in the documentation of the state economy — that is not the same as a general private-property right.

**Does not support:** projecting Cylinder rhetoric onto Persepolis payrolls, or claiming the archives prove modern individual title in land.

### 8.3 How the product uses this

The Cylinder page’s philosophical layer may **ask Jannatkhah’s questions** (individual ownership, limits on political authority, religion and liberty) as the project’s analytical frame, clearly labeled **philosophical interpretation**. ## 11. Kuhrt and the popular “first charter” claim

Not a starting URL from Ali, but required to separate layers:

- Amélie Kuhrt, “The Cyrus Cylinder and Achaemenid Imperial Policy,” *JSOT* 25 (1983), 83–97 — listed in Iranica and BM bibliographies. **Full article not accessed this pass.** Iranica already uses it to treat the Cylinder as imperial/building policy, not a unique edict of human rights.
- Popular UN/human-rights framing is **modern cultural meaning**. Historical layer: Babylonian building inscription in Marduk’s voice and Cyrus’s royal self-presentation.
